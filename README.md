Humanitarian Barge Aid System (HBAS) – Open Blueprint

Author: Jordan Morris (UK) · MIT License · 2025

A low‑cost, unblockable method for delivering bulk food aid to shores without ports, checkpoints, or permission.

1. Overview

A single barge carries four cranes and hundreds of pre‑tied dinghies loaded with food. Cranes lower chains of dinghies into the water. A small tug drone (or any powered boat) pulls the chain toward shore. Parallel crane operation creates two long floating food lines. One‑way fuel: the dinghies do not return.

Goal: Bypass land‑based blockades. Deliver food directly to hungry civilians. Low tech, low cost, high throughput.

2. Components

Item	Quantity (per barge)	Specification
Barge	1	50–100 m deck, shallow draft, anchor capable
Crane	4	5–10 ton capacity, electric or hydraulic, diesel gen
Dinghy (inflatable)	200–400	2–4 m, cheap (blown‑up, patched)
Pallet	200–400	Wooden, standard 1x1.2 m, loaded with dry food
Rope	200–400 lengths	5–10 m per dinghy, polypropylene, floating
Tug drone / boat	1	Small, low power, remote‑controlled or autonomous
Petrol	1 trip worth	For tug only (dinghies are towed, not powered)
3. Preparation (On Barge)

Load each dinghy with one wooden pallet of food (rice, flour, oil, canned goods).
Tie dinghies together in chains of 5–10 units, using 5–10 m rope between each.
Stack chains on deck near each crane, ready for deployment.
Attach lead rope from first dinghy of each chain to the tug drone.
4. Deployment (At Sea, Near Shore)

Anchor barge at safe depth (e.g., 10–20 m), as close to shore as possible.
Lower tug into water.
Crane 1 lifts first chain of dinghies, swings over water, lowers until floating.
Tug drone pulls chain slowly toward shore.
Crane 2 begins deploying its own chain immediately – overlapping, parallel operation.
Cranes 3 and 4 follow in sequence, maintaining two active chains at all times.
Result: Two floating food lines moving continuously toward shore. Tug drone returns after dropping chain, picks up next lead rope, repeats.

5. Shore Side (No Infrastructure Required)

Dinghies beach themselves on arrival (low tide best).
Locals can untie pallets, carry food inland, or drag dinghies clear.
Empty dinghies can be reused or left – they are cheap.
6. Operational Notes

Timing: Work during calm seas, slack tide.
Fuel: Tug drone needs enough petrol for multiple trips back and forth (one trip = one chain delivery).
Security: Operation is in international waters until chains reach shore. No armed escort needed.
Communications: Basic marine VHF. Drone control via line‑of‑sight or short‑range radio.
Failure tolerance: If a dinghy punctures, the rest of the chain still lands. Pallets float (wood).
7. Advantages

Problem	Solution
Blockaded ports	No port needed – barge anchors offshore
Checkpoints	No land crossing – food arrives by sea directly to civilians
Expensive infrastructure	Uses cheap dinghies, wooden pallets, tug drone
Slow unloading	Parallel crane operation = high throughput
Political permission	None required (international waters, humanitarian aid)
8. Estimated Throughput (Per Barge, Per 10‑Hour Day)

Dinghies per crane: ~50
Dinghies per day (all cranes): ~200
Food per dinghy: 200–500 kg
Total food per barge per day: 40–100 tonnes
Multiple barges = multiple streams.
